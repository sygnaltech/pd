# Brand Voice — Ponsonby Doctors

A working style guide for English content across the Ponsonby Doctors website and CMS. Applies to service descriptions, doctor bios, articles, alerts, clinic info, and any patient-facing copy. Use this as the source of truth when writing new content, editing existing content, or generating drafts.

---

## The clinic, in one paragraph

**Ponsonby Doctors** is a multi-disciplinary medical practice in Ponsonby, Auckland, New Zealand. The practice operates several sub-clinics under one roof — general practice, Cosmetic Skin Clinic, Immigration Clinic, Corporate Health Clinic, and others — and serves a culturally diverse patient base. Staff are fluent across English, Mandarin Chinese, Korean, Filipino/Tagalog, and Indonesian. The brand sits at the intersection of warm primary care and credentialed clinical expertise.

## Self-reference

- **Full name:** Ponsonby Doctors. Always capitalised, never hyphenated.
- **Short form:** "PD" is acceptable in informal contexts and inside long-form articles where the full name has already been introduced (e.g., *"talk to your doctor at PD"*). Do not use "PD" in headlines, meta descriptions, or first sentences.
- **Pronoun:** "we" / "our" when speaking as the clinic. Never "I".
- **Patients:** call them **patients** — not customers, clients, or users. The one exception: cosmetic content may say "you" / "your skin" directly, which is consistent with current copy.
- **Domains:** ponsonbydoctors.co.nz is the primary; ponsonbydoctors.nz is the legacy domain. Refer to the .co.nz form in copy.
- **Email used in bios:** info@ponsonbydoctors.co.nz (matches current CMS data — change centrally if migrated).

## English variant — New Zealand English

**Use New Zealand / British spellings consistently.** Current CMS content is inconsistent on this point (some articles use "immunization", "personalize", "enrolling at"; cosmetic content correctly uses "revitalise", "ageing", "colour"). New and edited content should standardise on NZ English.

| Use | Not |
|---|---|
| immunisation, immunise | immunization, immunize |
| organisation | organization |
| personalise, personalised | personalize, personalized |
| revitalise, moisturise | revitalize, moisturize |
| ageing | aging |
| colour, behaviour, favour | color, behavior, favor |
| centre, fibre | center, fiber |
| enrol, enrolled, enrolment | enroll, enrollment |
| paediatrics, paediatric | pediatrics, pediatric |
| oesophagus, foetal | esophagus, fetal |
| -yse (analyse, paralyse) | -yze |

**Exceptions:** keep American spelling inside proper nouns, brand names, and direct quotes ("Cosmetic Skin Clinic" is a name; if a manufacturer's product literature uses "Color Booster", preserve it).

**Date format:** `8 June 2026` or `2024-12-08` in CMS date fields. Avoid `06/08/2026` — ambiguous between NZ and US readers.

**Currency:** NZD with a leading dollar sign, no space (`$50`, `$5 per item`). Note GST inclusivity explicitly when relevant (existing content does this — keep it).

**Numbers and units:** metric ("16 weeks", "20mg/ml"). Times in 12-hour with lowercase am/pm and a space: "10 am to 4 pm".

## Voice attributes

The voice is **warm, credentialed, calm, and inclusive**. Four levers to pull, with do/don't framing:

### 1. Warm, never breezy
We sound like a thoughtful clinician explaining something to a patient across the desk — not a marketer, not a friend at a bar.

- **Do:** *"This is a treatment for lips that feel dry, look dull, or have lost their natural fullness and definition over time."*
- **Don't:** *"Tired lips? We've got you. ✨"*

### 2. Credentialed, never cold
Use precise clinical language, but pair it with a plain-English unpacking the same sentence or paragraph.

- **Do:** *"Skinfill Bacio is a professional lip booster treatment that harnesses the power of Vitamin B12 and non-cross-linked Hyaluronic Acid (HA) to deeply hydrate, refine, and revitalise your lips."*
- **Don't:** *"A non-crosslinked HA biorevitalisation device with B12 co-formulation."* (too dense, no human framing)
- **Don't:** *"It's basically a fancy lip drink."* (too casual, undersells expertise)

### 3. Calm and honest about expectations
Never overpromise. Always couple a benefit claim with a realistic caveat where relevant — "results vary", "typically", "well-tolerated", "individual results will vary". This is both ethically required and on-brand.

- **Do:** *"Most patients experience little to no downtime. Some mild redness or swelling at the treatment site is possible but generally resolves quickly."*
- **Don't:** *"Zero downtime, guaranteed."*

### 4. Inclusive and practically aware
The patient base includes new migrants, families with young children, older adults, cosmetic patients, and corporate health clients. Don't assume cultural context, English fluency, or insurance literacy. Spell out NZ-specific concepts (PHO, enrolment subsidies, Community Services Card) when they appear.

- **Do:** *"When you enroll with a medical practice, you become part of their Primary Health Organisation (PHO). PHOs receive government funding to subsidise healthcare for enrolled patients..."*
- **Don't:** *"As you know, your PHO covers most of this."*

## Audience modulation

The same brand voice flexes across surfaces. Same dial settings on warmth and honesty; different settings on density and structure:

| Surface | Tone tilt | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Service pages | Informative, structured | Heavy use of H2/H3, bullet lists, FAQs. Pair every clinical term with plain English. |
| Doctor bios | Personal, biographical, warm | Narrative paragraphs. Lead with origin/training, include human detail (languages, family, interests). Address the doctor by first name only after first introducing them as "Dr. \[Last Name]". |
| Articles | Educational, sourced, calm under pressure | Public-health framing. Cite reputable sources (WHO, CDC, Ministry of Health, peer-reviewed). Quote pulled into blockquotes. End with a call to consult their doctor. |
| Alerts | Brief, urgent but not alarmist | One sentence. Active verb. e.g., *"Stay informed about whooping cough!"* |
| Clinic descriptions | Identity-forward | What the clinic is, who it serves, what makes it distinct (e.g., on-site x-ray, weekend availability, multilingual staff). |
| Cosmetic content | Aesthetic, sensory, restrained | Use sensory vocabulary (hydrated, supple, refined) but stay clinical underneath. Always include suitability and aftercare. |
| Booking notes & disclaimers | Direct, italicised, short | e.g., *"Please arrive 15 minutes early."* / *"Individual results will vary."* |

## Structural conventions for long-form content

Service pages and articles follow a recognisable scaffold. Stick to it unless there's a reason not to:

**Service pages — recommended sections, in order:**
1. Opening italic line — one sentence that captures the patient-facing benefit
2. Bold lede paragraph — what the treatment is, in two sentences
3. Plain paragraph — who it's for / what it helps with
4. `### What Makes [Treatment] Different?` (where applicable)
5. `### Key Benefits` — bulleted list, each item bold-leading
6. `### How It Works` — mechanism, in plain language
7. `### What to Expect` — the patient experience on the day
8. `### Treatment Highlights` or `### Aftercare`
9. `### Who Is a Good Candidate?` — bulleted list
10. `### Frequently Asked Questions` — bold question, plain answer
11. Closing italic — *"Individual results will vary. We recommend booking a consultation..."*

**Articles — recommended sections, in order:**
1. Optional blockquote — a quoted source or a one-line summary
2. Bold opener — the news/event/claim
3. Plain paragraph — why it matters to the reader
4. `### Who is at risk?` / `### What does it mean?` (framing question as heading)
5. Body sections answering the question
6. Closing sentence pointing to action: book a consultation, talk to a doctor at PD, see related services.

## Vocabulary preferences

**Prefer**

- *patients* (over customers/clients)
- *practitioner*, *clinician*, *doctor* (over "provider", "expert")
- *consultation* (over "appointment" when the visit involves assessment)
- *book* (over "schedule" or "make")
- *in-clinic*, *on-site* (hyphenated)
- *well-tolerated*, *minimal downtime*, *gentle*, *suitable for*
- *enrol*, *enrolment* (NZ spelling)
- *general practice*, *GP* (after first full mention)

**Avoid**

- *fix*, *banish*, *zap*, *destroy* (cosmetic content — too combative)
- *cheap*, *budget* (use *affordable* or specific pricing instead)
- *cutting-edge*, *revolutionary*, *game-changing* (clichés; if a treatment is genuinely new, say *recently introduced* or *innovative*)
- *guaranteed* in any clinical or cosmetic context
- *exclusive*, *VIP* — off-brand for a primary-care-led clinic
- *jab* (use *vaccination* or *immunisation*)
- *clients*

## Medical content guardrails

These are not optional. Any AI- or human-generated clinical copy must:

- Include a suitability statement when describing a treatment (who it's for, who it's not for).
- Avoid diagnostic language directed at the reader. Don't write "if you have X, you need Y" — write "if you experience X, consult your doctor about whether Y might be appropriate".
- Never quote prices without GST clarity and a "subject to consultation" qualifier where relevant.
- Cite sources for public-health claims. Prefer WHO, CDC, Te Whatu Ora / NZ Ministry of Health, peer-reviewed journals.
- Defer final clinical judgement to a consultation: *"A consultation with our practitioner prior to treatment will allow us to assess your individual needs."*
- For cosmetic treatments, always include the line *"Individual results will vary."*

## Multilingual context

Ponsonby Doctors publishes in English (primary), Chinese (zh), and Korean (ko). English is the editorial source — translations follow rules in [CMS-RULE.md](CMS-RULE.md). When writing English content:

- **Avoid idioms and culturally specific references** that don't translate cleanly. Prefer plain language.
- **Avoid puns and wordplay** in headings and CTAs — they break in translation.
- **Be explicit with NZ-specific concepts** the first time they appear (PHO, GST, Community Services Card, Care Plus) so translators have unambiguous source text.
- **Avoid contractions in formal copy** (write *"do not"* instead of *"don't"*) where it reads naturally; contractions are fine in conversational sections like FAQs and bios.

## Common scaffolds — copy-paste starting points

**Service page opener:**
> *Brief sensory or benefit statement — one italic sentence.*
>
> **\[Treatment name\] is a \[category\] that \[mechanism\] to \[outcome\] — \[differentiator\].**
>
> This is a treatment for \[symptom/concern\]. Whether you want to \[goal A\], \[goal B\], or simply \[goal C\], \[Treatment\] offers a \[adjective\] solution.

**Doctor bio opener:**
> Dr. \[First\] \[Last\] \[origin verb\] in \[country\] and obtained their Medical Degree from \[institution\] in \[year\].
>
> They \[career arc — hospitals, training, fellowships\] before \[joining PD / starting general practice / specialising in X\].
>
> \[First name\] speaks fluent \[languages\]. Outside of work, they enjoy \[interests\].

**Closing line for cosmetic / treatment pages:**
> *Individual results will vary. We recommend booking a consultation with our practitioner to discuss your individual needs and goals.*

**Article closer:**
> Ponsonby Doctors is here to support your healthcare journey. For more information, talk to your doctor at PD or \[link to relevant service/clinic\].
